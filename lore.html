The Lore of KnightZ

The Year the Sun Went Grey

Three of the oldest stories in the western world fall inside one generation: a sky that failed, a monster in a Danish hall, and the giants who lived before the Flood. KnightZ sets all three in the same place, in the same years, and asks what if each one was true.

History First

Every legend starts with something that really happened

In AD 536 the sun dimmed over Europe for more than a year. Ice cores, tree rings and the chroniclers of three empires all record it. The harvests failed, then plague came out of the Mediterranean and crossed to Britain. In the same years the Danes were building great halls and singing about a creature that walked the fens in the shape of a man. The monks who kept the annals were reading Genesis, and some of them knew an older book about giants and the angels who fathered them.

KnightZ: The Failing Light is built from those records. We took the real eruptions, the real famine entries, the real plague DNA, the oldest epic in the English language and the oldest surviving apocalypse, and laid them on a single timeline. They fit. The story we tell lives in the gaps between what was written down and what nobody dared to write.

That is why we hold the rest of the game to the same standard. If the chronicle says the bread failed, the fields in the game are thin. If a sixth-century Briton could not have owned it, worn it or built it, you will not find it in Powys in 548.

Kept to the period

  • The land: real terrain, real rivers, and only plants that grew in Britain in the sixth century.
  • The dress: wool and linen in layers, period footwear, colours from the dyes of the time.
  • The economy: grain, iron, livestock, and salt carried in along the old Roman road.
  • The arms: the spatha, the seax, the spear and the round shield.
I
AD 536 to 547

The Sun Put On Ashes

In 536 a cloud of volcanic dust spread across the northern hemisphere and stayed. The Byzantine historian Procopius, writing from the court of Justinian, said the sun shone without brightness, like the moon, for the whole year. In Ireland the annalists wrote three words for 536: failure of bread.

For a long time nobody knew the cause. Then the ice answered. Sulphate layers in cores drilled from Greenland and Antarctica show large eruptions in 536 and again around 540 (Larsen et al., 2008). A re-dating of the ice-core record in Nature in 2015 confirmed two great eruptions back to back: a northern one in 536 and a tropical one in 540, among the largest of the last 2,500 years.

The tree rings agree. A study of Alpine and Russian Altai trees found summers so cold after 536 that the authors named the period the Late Antique Little Ice Age, running from 536 to around 660, with a third eruption in 547. A climate study of the double event placed 536 to 545 among the coldest decades of the last two thousand years (Toohey et al., 2016).

Where the first eruption happened is still argued. Medieval historian Michael McCormick's team pointed to Iceland, as reported in Science, which named 536 "the worst year to be alive". The Ilopango volcano in El Salvador has been proposed for the second (Dull et al., 2019), though later dating work places that eruption a century earlier.

In the game

KnightZ opens twelve winters after 536. The sky is a milky veil over a pale, reddish sun, summers are short and cold, and clear blue sky is rare enough to be remembered.

"For the sun gave forth its light without brightness, like the moon, during this whole year."

How we know

Three independent records line up on the same years: sulphate in polar ice, narrow growth rings in old timber, and eyewitness writing from Constantinople, Italy and Ireland.

II
Britain, 536 to 549

Then the Bread Failed

Britain had no Procopius, but it had annals. The Welsh annals, the Annales Cambriae, set down 537 in one line: the battle of Camlann, in which Arthur and Medraut fell, and there was plague in Britain and Ireland. Ten years later they record the great death in which Maelgwn, king of Gwynedd, died. A later hand called it the Yellow Plague, the name it still carries in Wales.

The disease was the Plague of Justinian, the first pandemic of Yersinia pestis, the bacterium of the Black Death. It reached Constantinople in 541. In 2019 a team led by the Max Planck Institute recovered its DNA from burials at Edix Hill in Cambridgeshire and linked it to a pestilence in the British Isles around 544 (Keller et al., PNAS). The chroniclers were describing something real.

Hunger came first and plague followed a population already starving. Roman Britain may have held three to six million people (Härke). By the later sixth century the archaeology of the west goes quiet: a 2023 survey found a sharp and lasting fall in activity at the hillforts and grain-drying kilns of western Britain (Comeau, Seaman and Bloxam). Writers such as David Keys in Catastrophe argue that famine and plague together came close to emptying the British kingdoms, and opened the east of the island to the Saxons.

How many died is still debated, and some historians think the plague's toll has been overstated (Mordechai et al., 2019). What the record shows beyond argument is a decade of failed harvests, a pandemic, and kings dying of it.

In the game

Owain was orphaned in the famine. The manor he inherits has empty houses, untended strips and too few hands for the harvest. Keeping its people fed through five winters is the heart of the game.

III
Denmark and Britain, around 500 to 550

Beowulf and the Old People of the Hills

Beowulf survives in a single manuscript copied around the year 1000, but the story is set much earlier. Its one datable event is the death of King Hygelac in a raid on the Franks, which Gregory of Tours records around 516 to 521. A later Latin book of wonders, the Liber Monstrorum, claims Hygelac was so huge that his bones were kept on an island in the Rhine for travellers to see (Hygelac).

At Lejre in Denmark, the traditional seat of King Hrothgar, archaeologist Tom Christensen excavated a hall about 50 metres long, dated to the mid-sixth century (Heorot). By the poem's own chronology, Grendel's attacks on Heorot fall within a generation of 548.

And what was Grendel? The poem gives few details, but it is clear on one point. He walked on weres wæstmum, in the form of a man, only larger than any other man. He was strong enough to carry off thirty sleeping warriors, he lived with his mother in the fens and under the mere, and the poet calls him the kin of Cain.

Readers have long wondered whether a memory of another kind of human lies under the monster. Michael Crichton built his 1976 novel Eaters of the Dead, filmed as The 13th Warrior, on that idea, with the Grendel figures written as surviving Neanderthals. Writer Mark Carpenter makes the case in Beowulf and Grendel: A Genetic Interpretation. Medieval Europe was full of such figures: the hairy wild man of the woods, the wodewose of English carving.

The Neanderthals were real and they lived here. Teeth from Pontnewydd Cave in north Wales, about 230,000 years old, are the oldest human remains found in Wales. Their DNA survives in most people living today. Science dates their disappearance to around 40,000 years ago (Higham et al., Nature 2014). KnightZ asks what would follow if a few of them never left the high country.

In the game

The manor's folk fear the Uld, a heavy-boned people of the uplands. The Saxon traders have their own name for them, taken from a song about a Danish hall. How you treat the Uld decides how the story ends.

"On weres wæstmum wræclastas træd, næfne he wæs mara þonne ænig man oðer."

IV
Genesis, Enoch and the Anglo-Saxon poets

The Giants Before the Flood

The sixth chapter of Genesis holds four strange verses. The sons of God took wives from the daughters of men, and their children were the Nephilim, "mighty men which were of old, men of renown". Then the earth was filled with violence, and God sent the Flood. Yet the Nephilim appear again after it, when the spies sent into Canaan report giants in the land (Numbers 13:33).

The Book of Enoch tells the full story. Its first part, the Book of the Watchers, was written around 300 to 200 BC; Aramaic fragments of it were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Two hundred angels, the Watchers, descend on Mount Hermon, take human wives and teach mankind metalwork, war and sorcery. Their sons are giants who devour the labour of men and then men themselves. The Flood is sent to end them. The New Testament letter of Jude quotes Enoch directly, and the Ethiopian Orthodox Church still holds it as scripture. The Watchers section closes with a warning. The bodies of the giants drowned, and their spirits remained on the earth.

This tradition was known in the British Isles. M. R. James found a Latin passage of Enoch in an eighth-century manuscript in the British Museum. And Beowulf itself makes the link. The poet says Grendel descends from Cain, and from Cain's line came ogres, elves and the giants who "strove with God for a long time, and he paid them their reward." Scholar Ruth Mellinkoff showed that the poet was drawing on the Enoch tradition of the giants and the Flood, and on the medieval belief that some of them survived it (Part I, Part II: Postdiluvian Survival).

In the game

The monks of the manor have read Genesis. Some have read further. What the giants left behind in Britain is something you will have to find for yourself.

One generation

Where the Three Stories Meet

Set the dates side by side and the three stories collapse into a single lifetime. A boy born in 520 saw the sun fail at sixteen, buried his family in the plague years, and could have heard the song of Heorot from a Saxon trader before he was thirty. Every monk who read him the chronicle also knew the story of the giants and the Flood.

Recorded

The eruptions, the cold, the failed harvests, the plague, the death of Maelgwn, Hygelac's raid and the hall at Lejre all stand on documents, ice, timber or DNA.

Believed

The Nephilim and the Flood were history to every literate person in sixth-century Britain. The monks wrote them down as plainly as the weather.

Imagined

A last band of Neanderthals in the Welsh hills is our invention. Every other piece of KnightZ is built to sit beside the record without contradicting it.

Historical accuracy

Built to the Period

A story this close to the record only works if the world around it is right. We research every building, garment and trade good against what archaeology and the written sources say about western Britain in the 540s, and we cut what does not belong.

The Land and the Buildings

The map follows the real elevation of the Severn valley. Halls and longhouses are timber, wattle and daub under thatch. Stone is old Roman work, reused. Timber is cut in saw pits, since water-powered sawmills belong to a much later age.

Dress

Wool and linen worn in layers: tunics, kirtles, cloaks fastened with brooches, and turnshoes of leather. Colours come from the dyes of the period, such as madder, woad and weld, and the poor wear undyed cloth.

Arms

The long spatha for those who can afford one, the seax for everyone else, the spear and the round shield. Armour is rare and costly. A mail shirt is a fortune.

The Economy

Grain, livestock, iron and labour. Salt cannot be made in the hills, so it comes in by road from the brine springs at Droitwich. Names follow the Brittonic of the time, and the Saxons are traders and exiles, as the record suggests.
