Overview

Bedwyr's hair took 24 versions. The prompt that finally landed was one screenshot with two coloured circles and four plain sentences: what I saw, where I saw it, what it should look like, and why.

Episode 3 goes back through three weeks of KnightZ sessions and pulls out the prompts that made the biggest difference. In that time Claude helped build 16 hero characters with face rigs, imported them all into Unreal, generated hundreds of animation clips, and wrote a screenplay, a combat design and an economy. This page lists the actual prompts, what each one did, why it worked, the four that cost time, and two templates you can copy.

The prompt that fixed Bedwyr's hair

Version 1 of Bedwyr's hair cap came out of an overnight batch. Versions 2 to 23 came from text feedback. Version 24 was approved after this prompt and one marked-up screenshot.

Visual feedback prompt (Bedwyr hair)
I can see the skull mesh through the hair cap where circled in blue.
Your grey streaks run perpendicular to the hair grain where circled in red.
The grey streaks should run the same direction as the hair texture so they look like grey hairs.
The idea for the grey streaks is to make his hair more grey; it is too brown for an old man with a grey beard.
What it did: Claude mapped the circles back onto the 3D mesh, filled the hole, re-aligned the streaks and shifted the overall hair colour.
Why it worked: location (colour-coded circles), problem, desired result and the reason. The reason fixed a problem the circles never showed: the overall colour was too brown.

The session-start prompt: four parts

Every new AI session starts with no memory of yesterday. The first prompt has to carry the context. The best session starts all had the same four parts:

#PartWhat to include
1Where the work livesEngine and version, project path, file and object names. Claude stops guessing which file you mean.
2What to read firstThe project docs that hold the history. Naming three docs replaces pages of explaining.
3Numbered tasksOne fix per item, in order, each with the exact change wanted.
4The finish lineSave, commit, update the checklist. Without it you get a different idea of done every time.
Session start (animation review, Oct 12)
We are reviewing and editing the HY-Motion animation clips in Unreal
(KnightZ, UE 5.8.3, project D:/__KSUIE_Gaming/KnightZ_3D/KnightZ_3D).
Read these project docs first: SESSION_NOTES_20261012_anim_review.md,
ANIM_POSE_RULES_20261012.md, UE_ANIM_REVIEW_tool_20261012.md.

1. Apply the 4 "Needs edit" notes on Guard_Walk:
   - FL: trim the opening where he walks forward-right; start where he walks forward-left.
   - R: trim the opening half step left; start where he steps right.
   - L: keep the animation, scale root motion so the feet stop sliding.
   - BR: he moves back-left. Tell me the options before changing it.
   Edit clips in place, keep root motion, check shield clearance, save, commit to git.
   I think we generated four options for each animation on our first pass,
   so check whether one of the others is correct.

Keep responses brief and factual. No em dashes.
What it did: a brand new session fixed all four clips and committed them in three commits with no re-explaining.
Why it worked: exact path and engine version, docs to read, one numbered fix per clip, safety rules (edit in place, keep root motion), a gate on the one risky item, and a hunch.
Share your hunch. The line "I think we generated four options for each animation" sent Claude to the alternate takes, where it found the real cause: the motion generator ranks its four takes by how far they travel, never by direction, so wrong-way steps pass. Future runs now check path direction too.

The finished clips can be seen in Dev Log #20.

Feedback on visual work

Game development is visual. The AI can measure a mesh, but it cannot see what bothers you unless you say it.

Name a reference and the symptoms

The first look at Owain in Unreal got the feedback "he looks deformed". That gave Claude nothing to measure, so it came back with questions. The second try named a reference and four symptoms:

Owain in Unreal (Oct 7)
Compare this to our blender model. He is deformed. At idle he should be
standing up straight and look like our blender model that was approved.
His bones look like they have been repositioned so his chest is stretched,
his back is crooked, he is leaning forward and his toes go into the ground.
What it did: Claude measured all four symptoms and traced them to bone axes off by 76 to 104 degrees from Unreal's Manny skeleton. It rebuilt the rig with Manny's bone frames. Idle lean went from 16.3 cm to 10.2 cm (Manny: 9.5 cm).
Why it worked: "deformed" alone gives nothing to test. "Leaning forward, toes in the ground, compared to the approved model" gives a target and a measurement.

Mark it up

Take a screenshot and circle the problems, one colour per problem. Then add the reason (see the Bedwyr prompt above).

Plain words work

You do not need the technical term. A clear description of the look and the direction of change is enough.

Bedwyr skin (Oct 8)
His skin looks like dry leather, reduce the depth of the texture a bit
to make it look more natural and less dry and tanned rhino hidish.
What it did: became a standing rule for every head in the game: if skin reads like dry leather, use a softer copy of its normal map.
Formula for visual feedback: where, what is wrong, what it should look like, and why.

Limits, gates and fixed replies

An AI that can run Blender and Unreal can also break them. Four habits keep that in check:

HabitExample from KnightZEffect
Hard limits"Do not export FBX or touch Unreal until all 15 are approved."One line protects the whole downstream pipeline
Non-negotiables"We definitely want face bones."All 45 face, skirt and cloak bones survived the skeleton rebuild
Gates"Tell me the options before changing it."Claude laid out three choices for the back-left walk; one was picked and nothing was lost
Fixed reply wordsApprove, Edit or RevertFifteen characters became a repeatable loop
Review loop (hair caps, Oct 8)
For each hero:
1. Open the right blend. Show only body, head, eyes, lashes and the new hair.
2. Show me the review sheet plus the known issue for that hero.
3. Wait for my call: Approve, Edit, or Revert.
4. On Approve: tick the checklist, add a dated note, save, open the next hero.
5. On Edit: change the new hair object only, re-render as a new version, show it, wait again.

Rules:
- Do not export FBX or touch UE until all 15 are approved, unless I say so.
Why it worked: a fixed order, a fixed set of replies, what to do after each reply, versioned outputs, and a hard stop line.
Skeleton rebuild: check the record, name what must survive (Oct 7)
Please review our notes because I'm pretty sure you suggested a different skeleton than Manny. Review the notes while I pose the session working in blender... figure out why we didn't use a manny skeleton to begin with. But we definitely want face bones so figure that out also.
What it did: Claude found two earlier skeletons in the notes, showed that the old rule "never import onto Manny's skeleton" had no recorded reason, and built a Manny-conformed rig that kept every face, skirt and cloak bone.

The night run: decide first, then one word

For the overnight hair cap rebuild, Claude asked its planning questions first: which characters, Blender only or Unreal too, keep the old meshes, triangle budget, how to handle ears. The answers went into a project doc so an interrupted session could resume. Then:

The whole prompt
Go

Next morning: 15 new hair caps, each with a review sheet and a known-issues list. Every decision had been made before leaving, so nothing stalled overnight.

Four mistakes I made so you don't have to

MistakeWhat was missingCostFix now in use
Sent a big character build from my phoneBlender was connected through the desktop app; the phone session could not reach itSession stalled until reopened on the desktopStart tool-heavy sessions from the computer that runs the tools
A rule with no reason: "never import onto Manny's skeleton"Nobody wrote down whyA whole session working out the historyEvery rule lives in a doc with the reason and the measurement
Described a hair underside shape in wordsA clear picture of the target shapeGenerated version rejectedAsked for a flat plane, sculpted it by hand, had Claude measure the sculpt and turn it into a rule
Vague opener: "review the project files and you'll get an idea for the economy"A specific questionA general summary before the real answerAsk the direct question first
The direct question that worked
We have production of stone, but how are we consuming it?
Have we worked out that logic and that structure?
The pattern: when you can show it (sculpt, circle, screenshot), show it. When you can name the reference, name it. When a rule exists, write down why.

Every session writes the next prompt

The prompts got shorter over time, and better. At the end of every session Claude saves what was learned to the project: character records, scripts and rule docs (over 200 so far). Then it writes the prompt for the next session, ready to paste. The animation prompt above was written that way.

  1. Session prompt: where, what to read, numbered tasks, finish line.
  2. Claude does the work and renders review images.
  3. You review: Approve, Edit or Revert.
  4. Lessons saved: rules with reasons and measurements.
  5. Next prompt written into a NEXT_SESSION doc.
  6. Repeated procedures become skills.

When a procedure repeats, it becomes a skill. The question that started it:

Asking for judgement
Is it possible to build a subagent that does all of these hair edits we are working on?
Do you know how to do that? Would it make sense to do that?
What it did: Claude recommended a skill instead of the subagent named in the question, with reasons. Asking whether something makes sense got a better answer than the tool first named.

Now a new hairstyle or head starts with one line:

Skill call
/newhair Wulfstan

Skills in use: /newhead, /newfathead, /newhair, knightz-rig-model and Weight Pose Adjust. Episode 1 shows /newhead and /newhair step by step.

Templates you can copy

Session-start template
We are [task] in [tool + version], project [path].
Read these docs first: [doc 1], [doc 2], [doc 3].
Use [connector / tool] for [what].

Tasks, in order:
1. [Item]: [exact change wanted].
2. [Item]: [exact change wanted].
3. [Risky item]: tell me the options before changing it.

Rules:
- Do not [hard limit] until I say so.
- Keep [thing that must survive].
- Back up / version every change.

Finish: save, commit, update [checklist / notes], write the next-session prompt.
Hunch: [anything you suspect, even if unsure].
Keep responses brief and factual.
Visual feedback template
[Attach screenshot with circles, one colour per problem.]
Blue: [what is wrong there].
Red: [what is wrong there].
It should look like [reference: approved model, reference image, real-world comparison].
The reason: [why you want it].

Checklist before you send

  • Did I name the files, paths and versions?
  • Did I point to the docs that hold the history?
  • Is each fix its own numbered item?
  • Did I say what must not happen?
  • Did I gate the risky step behind "show me options"?
  • For visual issues: did I give a reference and a reason?
  • Did I say what "done" means (save, commit, notes)?

Ten rules for effective prompts

#RuleKnightZ example
1Say where the work lives"UE 5.8.3, project [path]"
2Point to the memory"Read these project docs first"
3Number the tasksFL / R / L / BR, each with its own instruction
4State the finish lineSave, commit, update the checklist
5Set hard limits"Do not export FBX until all 15 are approved"
6Gate the risky step"BR: tell me the options before changing it"
7Describe symptoms against a reference"Leaning forward, toes in the ground, compared to the approved model"
8Give the reason"Too brown for an old man with a grey beard"
9Mark it upBlue for skull showing, red for streak direction
10Fix the reply formatApprove / Edit / Revert; "brief, factual, no em dashes"
